# The fiber cutter
{: .no_toc }

<p class="vz-lede">A loop of polyester packing strap running on cheap rods about 32 in off the ground, with a small plastic fin every 4 ft. A fiber FPV's line falls across whatever lies under the drone's path, so where the drone crosses, its line lands <strong>on our strap</strong>. When the tripwire hears it, the strap starts moving: a fin grabs the line and carries it into a guarded blade at the next rod, <strong>with nobody exposed</strong>.</p>

<details class="layer" id="short-answer" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">Short answer</span><span class="layer-sub">The whole page in eight bullets</span></summary>

- **The idea is sound, and others have done half of it.** A fence that hears a drone and breaks its line already exists twice in Ukraine: a turning barbed wire, and Logika's Chaharnyk ([Countermeasures]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link countermeasures/index.md %}#whats-already-out-there)). Ours is a moving loop of packing strap, started by the tripwire's alerts.
- **The strap makes sure the line is on it.** A drone pays out its line with no tension from 65–165 ft up, so where it crosses the strap, the line lands across it. Nothing has to work out where the fiber lies.
- **The line is slack, so the strap has to grab it.** Rubber alone can't: nothing presses the fiber onto it. Instead each fin has a V slot that narrows below the fiber's width. The drag of the line's own legs pulls it into the slot, and the wedge holds it tighter the harder it pulls.
- **The strap powers the cut, not the fiber.** A blade at each rod crosses the slot's throat. The fiber's tension plays no part, and the strap never meets the edge.
- **A cut takes about 9 s on average and 17 s at worst** (a fin within 4 ft, then up to 50 ft to the next rod at about 2 mph). Slower than the old trolley design, much cheaper.
- **Parts cost about $995 a mile in a small batch.** Upkeep is about $150–300 a mile a year, plus mowing a strip about 3 ft either side (est.).
- **Set up by hand, like a farm fence:** about a mile a night for a crew of two (est.). The fins go on in the shop.
- **Two rules:** never cut our own fiber (ours crosses in a sleeve at an idler post), and hold the strap while our own fiber drones are out.

<details class="layer" id="why-it-lands-on-the-strap" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">Why the line lands on the strap</span><span class="layer-sub">The fiber's path, and where it doesn't hold</span></summary>

The fiber runs without a break from the pilot to the drone, and it falls from 65–165 ft up. So wherever the drone crosses our strap, the line must pass either over the strap or under it. Falling from above, **it ends up lying across the top of the strap.** That's why this design strings a strap rather than hunting for a line lying somewhere on the ground: the strap decides where the line will be.

**Where that fails:**
- **In tree belts.** A line that crosses in the canopy hangs in the branches, well above a 32 in strap. Put the cutter across open fields and leave gaps at windbreaks. A drone that flies down a tree belt slips through.
- **Wind** can carry a slack line sideways as it falls, but not over the whole length of the strap. It still has to cross the strap somewhere.
- **Old line already on the ground** from earlier drones lies under the strap, not over it, and does no harm.
- **Not yet tested:** whether a line laid from a real drone, over real grass, in wind, ends up lying on a 32 in strap. This is test C1 ([below](#what-has-to-be-tested)).

</details>

<details class="layer" id="the-grab" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">How a fin grabs a slack line</span><span class="layer-sub">Why rubber fails, and how a wedge holds</span></summary>

**The line is not taut, so something has to grab it.** A coated fiber is about 0.010 in across and weighs about 0.06 g a yard. The legs either side of the strap pull at about ½–2 oz in the air, and much less once they lie in grass.

**Rubber alone fails.** Friction is grip times how hard the fiber is pressed on, and nothing presses it on. Even very grippy rubber gives almost no hold, and it clogs with dust and ice and cracks in sun.

**A wedge slot grabs when the strap moves,** the way a rope jam cleat does. Each fin has a forward-facing V, about ½ in wide at the mouth, narrowing to a throat thinner than the fiber (about 0.008 in). When the strap moves, the line's legs drag on the ground and hold it back, so the fin slides under it and the fiber runs down the V into the throat.

**The wedge multiplies the pull** (model):
- 300 ft of line dragging over grass pulls back about 0.1 oz a leg.
- With 5° sides, the wedge turns that into about 0.6 oz of pinch each side.
- It locks: the sides are shallower than the friction angle (tan 5° = 0.09, against friction of about 0.3), so the harder the fiber pulls back, the tighter it holds.

**The grip only has to be small,** because the cut uses the strap's power. The grab never fights the cut. It only has to stop the fiber slipping out until it reaches the blade.

**The weak point:** whether 0.1 oz of drag reliably pulls a line *into* the slot in the first place. That can't be settled on paper. It is the first thing test C2 checks.

</details>

<details class="layer" id="the-design" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">The design</span><span class="layer-sub">Strap, loops, rods, fins, blades and posts</span></summary>

**The strap is ½ in polyester packing strap,** 0.028 in thick, rated 800 lb. A 6,500 ft coil costs about $119 by the skid, about 1.9¢ a foot ([Zoro](https://www.zoro.com/i/G9880622/), [iPack](https://www.ipack.com/smooth-polyester-strapping-027-x-6500-ft-p-353829)). A flat strap can't roll, so every fin points the same way, and it stretches far less than nylon (model):

| | Polyester strap | Nylon mono (the earlier design) |
|---|---|---|
| Stretch per 1,320 ft loop at working tension | 1.6 ft at 20 lb | about 37 ft |
| Change over a 100°F swing | 1.2 ft | more |
| Sag between rods 50 ft apart | 1.6 in | |

**Loops.** Each 660 ft loop runs out and back: the top run about 32 in up, outer face up; the return run a few inches below it, outer face down. A strapping seal closes each loop. A mile is 8 loops.

**Drive posts,** one every 1,320 ft, sit in the middle of two loops: a T-post carrying a 12 V car power-window motor (worm-geared and sealed) turning an 8 in printed pulley for each loop. They drive the strap at about 3.1 ft/s (90 rpm).

**Idler posts** at each loop's far end: a T-post with a printed pulley head and a hanging weight that holds 20 lb of tension and takes up about 4 ft of stretch and heat.

**Rods,** one every 50 ft: 3/8 in fiberglass fence rods with a printed head carrying two rollers on steel pins. The return run's roller is split so the down-facing fins pass through its middle. Rollers matter: with plain slots the drag would need 37 kg·cm at the motor, more than the 30 it has. With rollers it needs about 17.

**Fins,** one every 4 ft: stiff printed PETG, snapped through holes punched in the strap. Each has the V slot ([above](#the-grab)) and a groove the blade passes through.

**Blades,** one per rod: a segment snapped off an 18 mm utility blade, held in a guarded channel beside the fins' path at the height of the slot's throat, above the strap. Each fin passes the blade through its groove. Only something held in the throat meets the edge. The fiber breaks at about 2–13 lbf, and the strap can put about 1 lbf of extra pull into a cut (est., test C2).

**It reports breaks.** The drive box watches its motor's current. A sudden drop means a loop has broken, and the box says so over the radio.

</details>

<details class="layer" id="how-fast" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">How fast it cuts, and where to put it</span><span class="layer-sub">Seconds to the cut, the distance a drone covers meanwhile, and what that means for placement</span></summary>

A line that lands on the strap waits for the next fin, up to 4 ft away, then rides to the next rod, up to 50 ft on. At 3.1 ft/s (model):

| Rods 50 ft apart | Mean | Worst | Drone at 43 mph travels meanwhile |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fins every 4 ft (the design)** | about 9 s | 17 s | about 570 / 1,084 ft |
| Rods every 60 ft | about 10 s | 20 s | about 640 / 1,270 ft |

Rods every 60 ft save about $12 a mile. Fins every 3 ft cost about $35 more and save about 20 ft of standoff.

**What that means for placement:**
- **Drones waiting by a road: no limit.** The road guard's posts hear every landing within 300 ft of the road ([Guarding a road]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link models/route-guard.md %})). A strap about 330 ft out on the enemy side lies between every such drone and its pilot. Nothing is in a hurry.
- **Drones flying in: more than 1,100 ft in front of what it protects,** plus however far the drone flies while its line falls and the trigger arrives. A drone that goes dark should come down short of its target, but it falls with its warhead, so the strap also has to sit far enough from people. A drone with a backup radio keeps coming, onto the radio and the jammers.
- **Run early.** The tripwire hears a drone about a mile out and names the stretch, so the strap there is already moving when the line lands.
- **Hourly clearing strokes,** one rod spacing each, cut lines from drones that landed unheard. A box that stops hearing the trigger's heartbeat runs clearing strokes more often, so jamming the link slows the cutter but never stops it.

</details>

<details class="layer" id="laying-it" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">Laying it</span><span class="layer-sub">Who sets it up, and how long it takes</span></summary>

**It goes up like a farm fence, by hand, from a vehicle, at night,** the same way the road guard's posts do. The fins go on in the shop, and the strap is wound back on reels. Per mile, for a crew of two (est.):
1. **Mark the line** on open ground about 330 ft out from the road, with a gap at each tree belt. About 30 min.
2. **Push in the rods:** 98 fiberglass rods, tapped in with a mallet, about 1 min each: about 1.5 h.
3. **Drive the posts and anchors:** 9 T-posts and 2 end anchors with guys: about 1 h.
4. **Run the strap** off its reels over the rollers, seal each loop and hang the weights: about 3 h.
5. **Fit the drive boxes,** pair each to the nearest road post and run a test stroke: about 1 h.

**That's about 7 h, about a mile a night for two people (est.).** A strap across the threat side of a 4 mi road is about four crew-nights.

</details>

<details class="layer" id="power" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">Power</span><span class="layer-sub">What a drive post uses, and its solar</span></summary>

Each drive post runs on a 12 V 20 W panel, a 12 V 7 Ah sealed lead-acid battery and a PWM controller with a low-voltage cutoff (model):
- **Running** two loops takes about 28 W, about 39 W while cutting.
- **A day of standby and hourly clearing strokes** uses about 5 Wh.
- **A December day of sun** gives about 21 Wh, leaving 16 Wh: **about 8 triggers a day**, at 2 Wh each (3 min of running).
- **The battery** holds 42 Wh above half charge: about 8 days with no sun, or 21 triggers.

</details>

<details class="layer" id="keeping-it-up" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">Keeping it up</span><span class="layer-sub">Blades, batteries, strap, grass, ice and shelling</span></summary>

A year, a mile, at small-batch prices (est.):

| Item | A year | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Blades | ~$10 | Glass dulls an edge. 98 segments swapped spring and autumn |
| Batteries | ~$25–35 | Sealed lead-acid replaced every 2–3 years |
| Strap | ~$70 | Polyester weakens in sun. A guess of every 3 years; not yet known |
| Shell damage | ~$50–200 | Strap, fins and rods; a pure guess |
| **Total** | **~$150–300** | plus labor |

**Not in the money:**
- **Grass.** A wedge that grabs a 0.01 in fiber also grabs a grass blade. The line needs a mown strip about 3 ft either side, or to run above what grows there.
- **Ice.** Freezing rain glazes the rollers and fills the slots. Not yet tested (C3).
- **A broken strap** is rejoined with a seal in minutes. The drive box reports the break.
- **A daily roll call.** Each drive post runs once a day and reports, so a jammed loop shows up the same day.

</details>

<details class="layer" id="our-own-fiber" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">Our own fiber, our own drones, and people</span><span class="layer-sub">What the cutter must never cut, and keeping people and animals safe</span></summary>

- **Our tip darts' fiber.** On a road, every 2 mi a tip dart's fiber runs from the fence to a post ([Guarding a road]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link models/route-guard.md %})). It must cross the strap in a rigid sleeve at an idler post, where no fin can reach it. The crew lays the cutter after the fiber and puts an idler post where the fiber is.
- **The unit's own fiber FPVs.** Any of our fiber drones flying out past the strap leaves its line across it. **The cutter can't tell their line from the enemy's.** Two rules: the strap only runs on alerts for drones heard *inbound* (the tripwire's [inbound-only rule]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link models/tripwire.md %}#solving-the-field-limits-at-no-extra-cost), proposed, T15), and a **hold** set from the checkpoint stops all strokes on a stretch while our own sorties are out. Troops already cut every line they find, friend or foe. A cutter that holds while our own sorties fly does better than that.
- **People and animals.** The blade sits in a guarded channel at the fins' throat, so a finger can't reach it, and the strap moves at about 2 mph. Mark the line, keep it off paths and gates, and keep it out of livestock fields.
- **Our own movement.** A strap across a field blocks our own vehicles and people, which is one critique of Chaharnyk too. On a road it runs beside the road, not across it, with a gap wherever a track crosses. Drones can slip through the gap.

</details>

<details class="layer" id="what-it-misses" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">What it misses</span><span class="layer-sub">Drones it can't stop</span></summary>

- **Radio-controlled FPVs,** which have no line. Jammers and the tripwire's warning are the answer there.
- **Fiber drones with a backup radio.** The cut forces them onto the radio, where the unit's jammers must take them down. With no jammer near, the cut does nothing.
- **A drone flying around the end** of the strap, or down a tree belt, or through a gap.
- **A drone flying in fast, close to its target.** It can cover 1,100 ft before its line is cut.
- **A line that crosses through the trees,** above the strap.
- **A pilot who sees the strap** and lands short of it, or another drone that cuts it. Like any fence, it shows where we are.

</details>

<details class="layer" id="what-has-to-be-tested" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">What has to be tested</span><span class="layer-sub">Three tests that decide whether to build it</span></summary>

These are proposed, numbered C1–C3, apart from the tripwire's T-tests.

| Test | What | Pass | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **C1. Does the line land on the strap?** | Fly a drone that pays out a real FPV fiber, about 100 ft up, across a 32 in strap over long grass. 20 passes, calm and in a 10–15 mph crosswind | The line lies across the strap, with no gap, in 18 of 20 passes | One spool, about $200–500, plus a borrowed fiber FPV kit |
| **C2. Does a fin grab it, and the blade cut it?** | On a bench: a few feet of strap with fins pulled past one rod and blade. Lay fiber across it, with each leg dragging about 0.1 oz, 100 times. Then 1,000 fin passes to check the blade clears | 98 of 100 grabbed and cut. The blade still clears and cuts after 1,000 | Strap, a few fins, one blade, about $50 plus fiber |
| **C3. Does it run through a winter?** | One loop outside from December to March, clearing hourly. Log every stroke, ice, snow and grass | 95% of strokes run. The battery never runs flat | ~$300 |

**What would scrap it:** in C1, the line hangs above the strap or slides off it in wind most of the time. Or in C2, a fin can't reliably grab a line that lies loose, with no tension on it. The fallback is Chaharnyk's approach: drag hooks to pull the line tight before it breaks.

</details>
